Process engineering · by Facundo Luluaga

People don't resist change.
They resist changing what they can't see.

Most industrial projects aren't lost on the technology. They're lost on an operation nobody can fully see — and a change that gets handed down on top of it.

read on

The idea this site runs on

Walk onto a factory floor and the resistance is everywhere — the operator who won't trust the new system, the team that nods at the new procedure and keeps doing it the old way. It's easy to call that people being difficult. Usually it isn't. They're being asked to change an operation nobody can fully see: processes that live in someone's head, data nobody trusts, output that depends on who's on shift. Changing what's invisible is a gamble, and they know it. The fear is rational.

So I don't start with the change. I start by making the operation legible — turning what people do into something visible, shared and repeatable. That's what standardization really is, and it's why it comes first: you can't safely change what you can't see, and you can't automate what you haven't standardized. The technical half has right answers you can buy. The human half — the one that gets the least planning and carries at least as much weight — is where projects are actually won or lost, usually at the seam where a sound system meets the people who never trusted it.

// what the research says

In manufacturing tech adoption, the organizational factors outrank the technology itself.

STRATEGY 21.3% ORG 21.0% MGMT 20.9% TECH 19.0%
Sukathong et al., 2021 · SEM · n=212 SMEs

The famous “70% of change fails” figure has no valid empirical basis — the sources just cite each other.

Hughes, 2011 · J. of Change Management

Of 336 Industry 4.0 manufacturing studies, only 37 examined the human side at all.

37 / 336
Reiman et al., 2021 · scoping review

Whether improvements stick is decided 9–18 months out — the window almost no one keeps measuring.

Glover et al., 2011 · 65 Kaizen events

Not “people matter, technology doesn’t.” The two interact — and most failures happen at the seam between them.
