Knowledge · how I think about it

Not definitions. Frames.

The methods are on the web; anyone can look up what OEE or SMED means. What's worth writing down is the angle — why these things fail, when they apply, what they hide, and how I read them on a real floor. Each entry leads with that, ties to a case, and only then points you to the source.

Frames

The core — how I read a floor before reaching for a tool.

F1decision-making

Cynefin — choose the method by the domain

Most "method failures" are domain errors. A complicated-world tool — study it, find the answer — used on a complex problem (emergent, probe-sense-respond) fails not because the method is bad, but because it's in the wrong domain. Knowing the domain is the first decision, before any tool.

In short

Snowden's framework sorts a situation by the kind of cause-and-effect at play, and prescribes a different way of acting in each.

F2the site's spine

Legibility first — standardize → automate

It's an order, not a menu. Standardized data is the precondition for automation, digital twins and trustworthy reporting; skip to automation and the project sits on sand. And resistance to a mandated standard is rational when the thing being changed is still invisible.

In short

You can't improve, standardize or automate what you can't see. Standardization is the act of making an operation legible.

F3knowledge

Tacit vs explicit — why the manual fails

A standard is an attempt to externalize tacit knowledge — always partial. The drift between the documented method and the real one is usually tacit knowledge correcting the explicit version. The retiring expert is tacit knowledge walking out the door.

In short

Explicit knowledge can be written; tacit ("we know more than we can tell," Polanyi) resists codification. SECI describes the conversion.

F4metrics

Vanity metrics — what OEE hides (Goodhart)

The definition is commodity; the value is knowing how it's gamed. Rework classed as process never touches OEE — the unit still counts as good — yet bleeds into cost. A green OEE can hide the exact loss it's meant to expose. A metric is only as honest as the categories under it.

In short

OEE = availability × performance × quality. Goodhart's law: once a measure is a target, it stops measuring well.

F5where projects fail

The sociotechnical seam

Failures live at the interface between the technical design and human adoption. Not "people win, tech is easy" — joint causation, with the failure in the overlap. The human/organizational factors weigh at least as much as the technical ones, and get the least planning. Design for the seam.

In short

Sociotechnical theory (Tavistock): work is a joint technical-and-social system; optimize one half alone and you degrade the whole.

F6change

The 70% myth, and resistance as information

It's a phantom statistic — the sources cite each other in a loop, with no empirical ground. The field can't even agree how to measure change failure, which is itself a sign of a complex domain. So resistance gets reframed: when you're asked to change something invisible, distrust is rational. Don't defeat it — remove its cause.

In short

The cited "70% of change fails" has no valid empirical basis; the original author disowned it.
